\section{Introduction}

Finding the right word is a familiar part of writing. Writers use thesauruses not only to find exact synonyms, but also to search for words that are more specific, less clichéd, or carry different connotations \cite{gero2019stylistic}. Unexpected suggestions can also shape the direction of the writing itself \cite{gero2019stylistic}. Word search can therefore serve more than one purpose: it can help a writer refine an existing expression, but it can also expose alternatives that lead the writing somewhere new.

Computational approaches to word suggestion have largely developed around lexical substitution, where the task is to find alternatives for a target word that preserve its meaning and remain appropriate in context \cite{zhou2019bert,omarov2023grounding}. Even within this formulation, these requirements are not identical. A candidate that fits the surrounding sentence can diverge from the meaning of the original word, motivating methods that balance target semantics with contextual information \cite{zhou2019bert}. 

For creative writing, however, preserving the original expression is only part of the picture. Work on creativity support shows that generated material can contribute without functioning as a direct replacement. Singh et al. describe ``integrative leaps,'' where writers did additional work to incorporate generated material of varying semantic relevance into developing stories \cite{singh2022elephant}. The semantic distance and direction of word recommendations have also been shown to affect subsequent idea generation
\cite{wise2024sparking}. Together with observations of thesaurus use \cite{gero2019stylistic}, this suggests that a lexical suggestion can be valuable both for how well it fits what a writer has already expressed and for the alternatives it makes available to explore.

This makes it useful to look past a single notion of a ``good'' lexical suggestion. We characterize suggestion sets along three dimensions: \emph{semantic alignment}, or how closely suggestions preserve the existing expression; \emph{linguistic plausibility}, or how probable and well-formed they are in context; and \emph{exploratory range}, or how far a set extends beyond semantically similar alternatives. Different lexical suggestion methods may occupy this space distinctively, without any one profile necessarily being preferable for writing.

What a method suggests also depends on the context it receives. In lexical substitution, surrounding context is used to identify substitutes appropriate to a particular occurrence and to preserve sentence meaning \cite{zhou2019bert,seneviratne2022cilex}. Context-sensitive word search similarly uses surrounding language to rank alternatives for writers \cite{wiegmann2022language}. Creative writing provides context at several possible scopes, from the immediate sentence to a broader passage or information about the writer's language. Rather than treating context only as a way to improve substitution quality, we explore how changing the information available to a method changes the profile of suggestions it produces.

Nevertheless, these computational profiles cannot show us how writers will react to a suggestion. A semantically distant candidate might prompt a new direction or prove unhelpful, while a closely aligned candidate might provide the refinement a writer is looking for. Prior work also shows that writers can adapt and build upon generated material rather than accepting it outright \cite{singh2022elephant}. We therefore pair our computational comparison with a writer study to examine how suggestions with different profiles are used, rejected, adapted, and built upon.

Against this background, we ask:

    \setlength{\parskip}{0pt}

    \item[\textbf{RQ1}] How do lexical suggestion methods differ in semantic alignment, linguistic plausibility, and exploratory range?
    \item[\textbf{RQ2}] How do different forms and scopes of writing context shift these profiles?
    \item[\textbf{RQ3}] How do writers use, reject, adapt, and build upon suggestions with different profiles?
\end{itemize}

To address these questions, we compare lexical-resource, masked language-model, and generative language-model approaches under different context conditions. We first characterize the suggestion sets they produce computationally, then investigate how writers respond to suggestions with different profiles.
